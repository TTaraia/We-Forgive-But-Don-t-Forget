A note before we begin | For young viewers | view=eritrea; style=memorial
This video remembers civilians who suffered during Eritrea's thirty-year war of independence, from 1961 to 1991. It is based on reports by Human Rights Watch and on historical research. We avoid graphic detail, but these events are sad. Numbers are estimates, and sources often disagree, so we say where figures come from. If you are young, please watch with a parent or a teacher.
-----
The first shot by Hamid Idris Awate | 1 September 1961 | view=eritrea; style=memorial
On the first of September, 1961, Hamid Idris Awate and a small band of fighters fired the first shots of the armed struggle, in the Barka lowlands. Human Rights Watch writes that in the years that followed, the army's methods were aimed less at the fighters than at villages suspected of helping them. Even an Ethiopian officer who strongly supported unity later criticized this, writing that the army had entered Eritrea with the attitude of a conqueror.
-----
Barka burns | February - April 1967 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Tessenei
On the 11th of February, 1967, about 5,000 Ethiopian soldiers began burning villages in the Barka lowlands, supported by artillery and aircraft. By April, Human Rights Watch reports, 62 villages had been burned. Local leaders counted 402 civilians killed, and about 60,000 cattle and camels were slaughtered. On the 12th of February, 21 prisoners, mostly teachers and government employees, were executed in Tessenei prison. Thousands of families fled to Sudan. More than 25,000 refugees were registered there that spring, in the first operation of the United Nations refugee agency in the Horn of Africa.
-----
More villages destroyed | July - November 1967 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Massawa,Keren
In July 1967, soldiers burned the villages of Eilet and Gumhot in the eastern lowlands. Thirty young men were killed in a single house, and fifty-one people died in all. In November, about 7,000 soldiers began burning villages around Keren. Human Rights Watch says that nearly all the villages of the Senhit region, 174 in all, were destroyed, and that crops were burned and wells poisoned. The destruction then spread to the highland districts of Seraye and Akele Guzai, where 86 more villages were burned.
-----
A mosque near Keren | November 1970 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Keren
In November 1970, an Ethiopian general was killed in an ambush near Keren. In reprisal, soldiers raided villages in the area. Eritrean historians record about 120 people killed at Besikdira on the 30th of November. Human Rights Watch records 112 people killed in a mosque at a village near Keren that month, after soldiers told them they would be safe inside. Reports of the total range from 112 to about 200. Few records were kept, and survivors' accounts are the main evidence.
-----
Ona | 1 December 1970 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Keren
The next day, the 1st of December, soldiers attacked the village of Ona, on the edge of Keren, where many families who had fled the raids had gathered. Human Rights Watch estimates that about 625 people were killed. Eritrean historians give higher numbers, over 800. The village was burned. Against official orders, boy scouts from Keren went in to bury the dead. Eritrean accounts remember Ona as one of the worst atrocities of the war.
-----
State of emergency | December 1970 - January 1971 | view=eritrea; style=memorial
In December 1970, the military governor declared a state of emergency in Eritrea. Soldiers gained the power to arrest people at will and to move whole populations. Strips of land ten kilometers wide along the coast and the Sudan border were declared prohibited zones, where the army could fire at any target. On the 27th of January, 1971, about 60 civilians, most of them elderly, were killed in a mosque in the village of Elabored.
-----
Om Hajer | July 1974 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Om Hajer
In July 1974, after rebel assassinations, soldiers burned the village of Om Hajer near the Sudan border. They ordered the villagers to gather in the local stadium, and then opened fire. Human Rights Watch reports that at least 54 civilians were shot dead and 73 more drowned trying to cross a flooded river. About 4,000 people fled to Sudan. General Aman Andom, the new head of state and himself an Eritrean, apologized and promised compensation, which was never paid.
-----
Asmara | 31 January - 4 February 1975 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Asmara
On the 31st of January, 1975, the Eritrean fronts attacked Asmara, and the city stayed under government control. Over the following four days, Human Rights Watch reports, government soldiers went through the city and nearby villages killing civilians. Journalists confirmed 331 civilian deaths, and other sources claimed up to 3,000. About 100 people were killed in the village of Woki Debra, just outside the city. The government said 124 civilians died from stray bullets, and it executed 13 of its own soldiers for excesses.
-----
A year of destruction | 1975 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Agordat
The Eritrean People's Liberation Front estimated that the army partly or fully destroyed 110 villages in 1975. Eritrean sources also report massacres, with hundreds of victims, at Agordat on the 9th of March and at Hirgigo on the 17th of April. The army blockaded food from reaching the highlands, and its military administrator explained the policy with a saying: to kill the fish, first dry the sea. Food prices around Asmara rose more than twenty times.
-----
Counting the cost | 1977 - 1978 | view=eritrea; style=memorial
In March 1977, a naval unit killed between 100 and 160 civilians at the Red Sea village of Imberemi. In early 1978, the Ethiopian administration in Eritrea itself estimated the cost of the war so far: 13,000 soldiers, and between 30,000 and 50,000 civilians, killed or wounded. It also recorded more than 200,000 Eritreans forced into exile, over half of them in Sudan.
-----
The air war | The 1980s | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Nakfa
Through the 1980s, Eritrea's liberated areas were bombed from the air. In 1990, Human Rights Watch reported that the town of Nakfa had been completely destroyed by ten years of repeated air raids. An Ethiopian pilot captured in 1984 said the order was to bomb everything that moves. Markets, farming and travel moved to the night, so that people could avoid the planes. Villagers learned not to wear bright colors or light fires in the daytime.
-----
She'eb | 12 May 1988 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=She'eb
In March 1988, Eritrean forces won the Battle of Afabet. In April, the Ethiopian government expelled foreign aid agencies, and on the 14th of May it declared a state of emergency. On the 12th of May, 1988, soldiers and tanks surrounded the village of She'eb, in the Semhar region, and told the villagers to gather for a meeting. Human Rights Watch documented about 400 civilians killed there, and at least 100 more in nearby villages. About 110,000 people were displaced that year, and about 40,000 fled to Sudan.
-----
Afabet | 3 - 4 April 1990 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Afabet
On the 3rd and 4th of April, 1990, air raids struck the town of Afabet. Human Rights Watch records 16 people killed on the 3rd, and 51 killed with 125 seriously wounded on the 4th. A German aid worker, Dr. Rupert Neudeck, who saw an earlier raid there in May 1988, said there was no military target in the town, only civilians.
-----
Massawa under the bombs | February - April 1990 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Massawa
On the 11th of February, 1990, Eritrean forces captured the port of Massawa. From the 16th of February, the Ethiopian air force bombed it for eight days, hitting food stores holding about 50,000 tonnes of donated wheat meant for famine relief. About half was burned. The raids returned in April. On the 13th of April, a camp of families who had left the city was bombed, and at least 25 civilians were burned. On the 22nd of April, cluster bombs fell on a crowded street at dusk, killing about 50 people and wounding 110.
-----
Massawa: the damage | May - June 1990 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Massawa
Human Rights Watch reported in July 1990 that more than 100 civilians had died in the air raids on Massawa, and that more than 100 houses were destroyed. On the 1st of May, the government threatened to bomb any ship docking at the port, and a relief ship had to turn away to Port Sudan. The raids continued until the 10th of June, when international pressure led the government to allow relief shipments. Human Rights Watch concluded that the repeated raids were deliberately aimed at civilians.
-----
Asmara under siege | 1990 - 1991 | view=eritrea; style=memorial; mark=Asmara
After Massawa fell, the Ethiopian army was confined to Asmara and its surroundings until May 1991. People lived under a curfew, and anyone out after dark could be shot on sight. On the 9th of June, 1990, soldiers left their base and shot dead 31 young people on their way home from watching a World Cup football match, wounding at least 15 more. Civilians were arrested on slight suspicion of supporting the Eritrean fighters, and many were kept from leaving the city.
-----
24 May 1991 | 24 May 1991 | view=eritrea; style=independent; mark=Asmara
On the 24th of May, 1991, Eritrean fighters entered Asmara as the Ethiopian army collapsed. Human Rights Watch noted that, to the great relief of Eritreans, the city was captured intact, without widespread destruction or loss of civilian life. Eritreans celebrate this day every year. The formal declaration of independence followed in 1993, after a United Nations-observed referendum.
-----
Remembering | 1961 - 1991 | view=eritrea; style=memorial
No one knows the exact number of victims. Many were never counted, and records of destroyed villages are incomplete. By 1993, about 350,000 Eritreans were still living as refugees in Sudan. Human Rights Watch also documented abuses by the Eritrean fronts during the war, and the two main fronts fought each other in a civil war. This video focuses on the actions of the Ethiopian military toward civilians. Remembering the victims, and learning the facts, helps young people understand where their country's story comes from.

The most significant dates | 1952–1991 | view=eritrea; style=war;
The most significant dates during and immediately following the Ethiopian occupation of Eritrea
-----
The Federation Era | 1952–1962 | view=eritrea; style=federation;
The Federation Era was a turbulent 10-year period from September 15, 1952, to November 15, 1962, during which Eritrea was joined to the Ethiopian Empire as an autonomous unit under the sovereignty of the Ethiopian Crown
-----
The Federation Begins | September 15, 1952 | view=eritrea; style=federation; 
Following UN Resolution 390(A), Eritrea is officially federated with the Ethiopian Empire as an autonomous unit with its own parliament, constitution, and flag.
-----
Language Suppression | 1956 | view=eritrea; style=federation;
Emperor Haile Selassie bans the teaching and official use of native Eritrean languages (Tigrinya and Arabic), forcing Amharic as the sole official language.
-----
The Flag Lowered | 1958 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Asmara
The Ethiopian government officially lowers the Eritrean flag and replaces it with the Ethiopian imperial flag.
-----
The ELM is Founded | 1958 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Nakfa
The underground Eritrean Liberation Movement (ELM) is formed in Sudan to organize secret political resistance against Ethiopian encroachment.
-----
The ELF is Founded | 1960 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Nakfa
The Eritrean Liberation Front (ELF) is established in Cairo, shifting the independence movement toward an armed struggle strategy.
-----
The Armed Struggle Begins | September 1, 1961 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Nakfa
Hamid Idris Awate fires the first shots against Ethiopian forces at Mount Adal, officially starting the 30-year Eritrean War of Independence.
-----
Annexation and the First Phase of War | 1962–1974 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Nakfa
The formal annexation of Eritrea on November 15, 1962, marked the definitive collapse of the Federation Era and ignited the first major phase of the Eritrean War of Independence (1961–1974)
-----
The Federation is Dissolved | November 14, 1962 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Asmara
Under heavy military pressure from Haile Selassie, the Eritrean parliament is forced to dissolve itself, and Eritrea is officially annexed as Ethiopia’s 14th province.
-----
The EPLF Splinter | 1970 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Nakfa
Internal ideological rifts within the ELF lead to the creation of the Eritrean People's Liberation Front (EPLF), which eventually becomes the dominant rebel group.
-----
The Derg Regime and Escalation | 1974–1988 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Nakfa
The Derg Regime and Escalation phase (1974–1988) transformed the Eritrean War of Independence from a low-level provincial insurgency into one of the largest, most violent, and heavily conventional conflicts on the African continent.
-----
The Fall of Haile Selassie | September 12, 1974 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Addis Ababa
The Ethiopian Emperor is overthrown in a military coup by a brutal communist junta known as the Derg, led eventually by Mengistu Haile Mariam, who severely escalates the war in Eritrea.
-----
The First Strategic Withdrawal | 1977 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Nakfa
After capturing nearly all of Eritrea, the EPLF is forced into a tactical retreat to the northern mountains of Nakfa due to massive Soviet military intervention backing the Ethiopian army.
-----
The Red Star Offensive | 1982 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Nakfa
The Derg launches its largest military campaign to crush the EPLF at Nakfa but fails completely despite devastating casualties.
-----
The Road to Liberation | 1988–1993 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Asmara
The Road to Liberation (1988–1993) was the final, decisive chapter of the 30-year conflict. Following the monumental victory at Afabet in 1988, the Eritrean People's Liberation Front (EPLF) seized the strategic initiative. Leveraging the collapse of Soviet backing and coordinating with Ethiopian rebel allies, the EPLF dismantled the occupying Derg army, liberated the country, and officially secured Eritrea's formal independence via a internationally supervised referendum.
-----
The Battle of Afabet | March 17–19, 1988 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Afabet
The EPLF destroys the Ethiopian army's commands at Afabet, a decisive turning point that British historian Basil Davidson called the most significant victory in Africa since Dien Bien Phu.
-----
Operation Fenkil | February 1990 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Massawa
The EPLF launches a massive surprise amphibious and land assault to liberate the strategic port city of Massawa, cutting off the primary supply route for Ethiopian troops in Asmara.
-----
The Fall of Asmara | May 24, 1991 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Asmara
EPLF forces enter the capital city of Asmara, completely defeating the remaining Ethiopian garrison and ending 39 years of Ethiopian rule.
-----
The Independence Referendum | April 23–25, 1993 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Asmara
A UN-monitored referendum is held where 99.8% of Eritreans vote in favor of sovereignty.
-----
Official Independence | May 24, 1993 | view=eritrea; style=war; mark=Asmara
Eritrea officially declares its sovereign independence and joins the United Nations, exactly two years after de facto liberation.
